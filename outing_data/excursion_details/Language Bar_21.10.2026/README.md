Susane organises a Sprachbar (this semester known as Language Bar) every month, but somehow, I managed not to attend a single one last year. Since I've been trying to get out more and actually do things instead of spending all my time at home, I decided that October would be my month to finally give it a try.

And honestly? I'm glad I did.

The Sprachbar takes place in a small room over the place where we have our graduation ceremonies, called the Staff Café. There were three tables set up: one for German, one for English, and one for Spanish. Susane was at the German table, while another woman, whose name I think was Emily, was looking after the Spanish table. She gave us the Bamberg tour last year.

Most of us gathered around the German table with Susane, while two people sat at the Spanish table with Emily. There were snacks and drinks on the tables, so at least we had some emotional support for the German-speaking adventure ahead.


## Round 1: Introduce Yourself... and Try to Understand the Question

Let me introduce today's contestants!

> a small disclaimer: I have trouble remembering names. The following account contains the absolute best of my memory, so please bear with me!

Around the German table, we had me, Tanya, a guy from Italy, Diego, Gicaro, Susane, Hasin, a guy in a leather jacket (who I think was Korean; I am not being racist,in a coverstation he said is muttersprache _ . i couldn't understand him properly but i think he said korean.), and Hano (who was Japanese) in the clockwise order.

Diego arrived after Gicaro, who showed up sometime around 7:00–7:30 PM. Later, the Korean and Japanese guys left, and when the Spanish table people left, one guy moved over with some  snacks from the English table .

Quite an international gathering, if I say so myself.
The rules for the first game were simple. We'd pick a card, read the question written on it, and everyone would answer in turn.
Simple, right?

Well, there was just one little obstacle between me and victory: the German language.


### Question 1: What's Your Ideal Weekend?

Hano picked the first card and asked something along the lines of: *„Wie verbringst du am liebsten dein Wochenende?“*

Now, I didn't understand either *verbringen* or *am liebsten*. Two important words in one question. Excellent start!

But I managed to put something together: *„Am Wochenend spiele ich viele Videospielen und mehr schlafen.“*

Honestly, a perfectly accurate description of my weekend routine. No lies, no exaggerations, just pure documentary filmmaking.

One thing I noticed was that Hano spoke very quietly. Even though he was sitting right next to me, I could barely hear him properly. Understanding what he was saying was a whole other challenge.
The Korean guy, on the other hand, spoke so quickly that I couldn't understand a single thing he said. My ears were working overtime, and my brain had already submitted its resignation.

But hey, we were only getting started!

### Question 2: What Is Your Dream Job?

My turn to pick a card!

I read out: *„Was wäre dein perfekter Job?“*

Initially, I thought it meant, "What is my ideal job?" But when the Italian guy had trouble understanding the question and Susane explained it, I realised it was asking about what was your perfect job.

Time to give my answer.

*„Ich habe nie gearbeitet in mein Leben. deshalb habe ich kein perfekt Job.“*

I was also unsure whether to pronounce Job as **job** or **yob**. It's funny how even a simple word can suddenly become a major decision when you're speaking a foreign language.


### Question 3: Where Do You See Yourself in Ten Years?

Tanya picked the next card and asked:

*„Wenn alles so läuft, wie du dir es wünschst, was würdest du in zehn Jahren beruflich tun und wo würdest du leben?“*

Neither Tanya nor I understood the question completely. After a little discussion in English with Susane, I finally figured out what it meant.

Now, this was a question where I actually wanted to say a lot. Unfortunately, my German vocabulary is limitted by alot.

So I put together something like:

*„In zehn Jahren,ich möchte  mit mein Eltern leben und mit mein Bruder Familie. Und ich habe ein Job.“*


The final sentence, *„Und ich habe ein Job“*, came out a little later than the rest, as though I'd just remembered that having a job might be important to my future plans.

Everyone laughed.

Look, at least I have goals! Living with my family and having a job. A simple life, really. 


### Question 4: Meditation and Mindfulness

The Italian guy picked the next card: *„Welche Rolle spielen Achtsamkeit oder Meditation in deinem Leben?“*

I had to translate this one to understand it properly. Translation: What role do mindfulness or meditation play in your life?
My answer?  *„Ich habe kein Achtsamkeit oder Meditation in meinem Leben. Wenn mein Leben ist schwer, ich gehe ins Bett und schlafen.“*


This one was a hit!

Everyone seemed to enjoy my answer, and honestly, I stand by it. Some people meditate, some journal, some go for a walk. I simply close my eyes and hope tomorrow is a better day.

The fact that I spend so much of my life sleeping probably makes this answer even more believable and sad.


### Question 5: Tell Us About a Mistake You Learned From

Diego picked the next question: *„Ist dir mal ein Fehler passiert, von dessen Folgen du später profitieren konntest?“*

Essentially, it was asking whether I'd ever made a mistake that I later learned something valuable from.

Now, I had an answer ready in my head. During my bachelor's degree, I wasted a lot of time playing video games and sleeping. However I haven't exactly stopped doing those things. So I have not learned from my mistake yet.

I translated my thoughts using Google Translate, but by this point, Hano and the Korean guy had left. After Hasin finished answering, it was suddenly my turn, and I panicked.

My brain searched desperately for a German sentence. Anything. Just something that would get me through the question.

And then, out came this masterpiece:

*„Wenn ich zuersten komme nach Deutschland, ich nehmen ein Bus. Ich vergessen zu press die halt buttom, und the Tür nicht geöffnet. Jetzt ich kenn,zu press the button.“*

THE ROOM LAUGHED.

And honestly, fair enough.

Of all the mistakes I could have talked about, my brain decided that the most suitable life lesson was learning how to press a button on a bus. But look, I learned something useful! I now know that *Knopf* means button and *drücken* means to press.

**Achievement unlocked: Successfully learned how to use German public transport.**



### Question 6: Which Famous Person Would You Like to Meet?

Gicaro asked the next question: *„Welche berühmte Person würdest du gerne treffen?“*

Susane answered Barack Obama. Hasin mentioned a Korean singer and motivational speaker, although it took us a while to explain to Susane what an MC was.
Then it was my turn.

*„Ich möchte APJ Abdul Kalam treffen. Er war Präsident of  Indien und ein Scientist .“* 
scientest is called *Wissenschaftler* in Deutsch.

Tanya chose Narendra Modi, while the Italian guy mentioned a famous comedian from Italy. I can't remember Diego's answer, and Gicaro didn't have anyone in mind, so we eventually settled on a football player for him.


### Question 7: What Would You Ask Them?

Instead of picking another card, Susane asked us what we'd say if we actually met the famous person we'd just mentioned.

Hasin came up with something complicated about the country, the economy, or something along those lines.

Then it was my turn. I said: *„Ich will frage für ein Unterschrift?“*

What I wanted to ask was whether I could get an autograph. nfortunately, my sentence didn't make much sense, and Susane and Hasin didn't understand me at first. But once I said "autograph", they got it and burst out laughing.

Another point for Team Broken German!

Tanya was busy on her phone, so we had to explain the question to her again. While she was thinking about her answer, I suddenly remembered something I'd like to ask Narendra Modi.

I wanted to ask him how he makes good tea.

But instead of asking, "How do you make tasty tea?", I said: *„Kannst du ein Tee machen?“*

Which basically means, "Can you make a tea?" Tanya was the only one who understood what I was trying to say.

I'm beginning to think that my German sentences are like those mysterious messages in movies. Only one person in the room can decipher them, and everyone else needs subtitles.

And Hasin, I am sorry, but I genuinely cannot remember the question you asked! 

## Round 2: Two Questions, Two People, Double Trouble

By this point, the guy from the Spanish table had joined us. He was from South Africa. So now we had seven people at the table, and everyone was from a different country except Tanya and me, who were both Indian.

International friendships, cultural exchange, and me struggling to understand basic German. The full experience!

For the next game, Susane gave everyone two cards. We had to ask our two questions to two different people.

The South African guy went first, asking Tanya a question that I had to translate. It was something along the lines of: *How do you deal with setbacks?*

Tanya gave a nice answer, and then it was my turn to use my cards.

My first question was: *„Worauf freust du dich montags am meisten?“* I asked Susane this question. And then I couldn't understand her answer.

Yes, I successfully asked a question in German and couldn't understand the reply for it.  Classic me.

My second question was: *„Bist du eher ein Telefonierer oder ein WhatsApper?“* I asked Diego, and this time he was the person who didn't understand my question.


Someone asked me: *„Welche Jahreszeit magst du?“*
I answered: *„Ich mag jetzt - Oktober. Es ist nicht zu kalt und nicht zu warm.“*

This sparked a little discussion, with everyone sharing their opinions about the weather and seasons.

Personally, I think October is pretty great. You get to enjoy the autumn weather without having to deal with the worst of the winter cold.

I was also asked another question during this round, but unfortunately, my memory has decided not to preserve that particular piece of information.



At some point, someone asked Tanya who supported her in life.

She answered with friends, family, and the Kurz studentin (classmates).

And then I decided to contribute something. I said: *„Auch deine Nachwuchskräfte.“*

Except nobody understood me.
Not one person.
Susane, especially, refused to let the matter go. She kept trying to figure out what I was saying, and eventually, I had to show her my phone screen.

I honestly don't remember exactly what I was trying to express anymore, but whatever it was, my pronunciation had apparently made the word unrecognisable.

This is the problem with learning German. Sometimes, you know a word, you think you're saying it correctly, and then you discover that what comes out of your mouth sounds like an entirely different word.

The vocabulary might be in your brain, but the pronunciation is clearly living somewhere else.


We continued going around the table with more questions.

Hasin mentioned playing video games, and the South African guy asked her: *„Was spielst du zurzeit?“*

She mentioned Genshin Impact, and I immediately wanted to ask her more about it. Unfortunately, the game had to continue, and I had to keep my gaming-related curiosity to myself.


Then Susane asked Gicaro something like "What is the craziest thing you've done in your life?"

While Gicaro was thinking about his answer, I jumped in. *„Mein ist Komme nach Deutschland“*

Everyone laughed again.

At this point, I'm beginning to suspect that my answers are the main source of entertainment at this Sprachbar. I might not be the most fluent German speaker in the room, but at least I'm keeping everyone amused.

Gicaro eventually told us a story about buying a lamp through Kleinanzeigen. Apparently, he had to travel to two different cities or something along those lines to get it. I don't remember all the details, but it sounded like a surprisingly elaborate adventure for a lamp.

Meanwhile, someone asked the South African guy about a favourite celebration in his hometown. He answered in proper German, explaining that there was a festival where children wore something like a pelt and went out, along with another festival around the same time when adults wore traditional clothing to school or work.

I understood perhaps 20% of what he said.
But hey, 20% is better than 0%.

And that, ladies and gentlemen, is how you measure progress at a German language café. Not by how much you understand, but by how much you can piece together before the conversation moves on.


## Final Round: Snacks, Goodbyes, and a Little Life Update

After everyone had asked their two questions, we wrapped things up. We cleaned the table, helped ourselves to some leftover nuts, and I took home the remaining grape juice.

Before leaving, I finally managed to ask Hasin a question of my own! *“Was ist dein Liebling character in Genshin?”* 
Sie anwortet, *“Venti! Und ihr?”*
Ich anworte, *“Meiner ist Kazuha.”* 

Then I asked her if she spoke English, and she said yes. So, I took this as my opportunity to tell her about the moment Kazuha blocked the Raiden Shogun’s Musou no Hitotachi — and how that was the moment I fell in love with him.It was fun to remember genshin and talk about it with someone.

Then Tanya and I said goodbye to everyone and walked back towards the dorms together.

We ended up talking about life along the way.
Tanya has completed her degree and is now looking for a part-time job because she needs one for her visa extension. Meanwhile, I told her that I should be looking for an internship, but instead, I've been sleeping for most of the day and not doing much else.
A very productive comparison of our current life situations, clearly. 
We eventually reached the point where we had to go our separate ways. Tanya lives in Haus Sachsen, while I live in Haus Coburg. Before saying goodbye, I told her that if she ever planned to do anything fun, she should let me know. I'm always at home anyway, so I'd be happy to join.

Then I wished her *Gute Nacht* and headed back.

I left the room at around 6:20 PM and got back around 8:50 PM.


## Final Score: German 1, Me 0... But I'm Getting There!

Looking back, the evening was genuinely fun.

I spoke a few simple sentences in German, managed to answer a bunch of questions, learned some new vocabulary, and even made people laugh along the way.

Did I understand everything? Absolutely not.

Did I sometimes have to translate questions, guess the meaning from context, or rely on Susane to explain things in English? Yes.

Did I confidently ask a question in German only to have the other person fail to understand it? Also yes.

But I showed up, participated, and tried. And I think that counts for something.

The biggest takeaway was realising just how much German I still need to study if I want to understand what people are saying in normal conversations. It's one thing to memorise vocabulary or practise sentences on an app, but it's a completely different experience when someone is sitting in front of you, speaking naturally, and expecting you to respond.

Still, I don't want to let that discourage me. If anything, the evening showed me exactly where I need to improve.

And the best part? Nobody seemed to mind my broken German. Everyone was patient, we laughed together, and I got to spend an evening meeting people from different countries.

I even got to take home half a bottle of grape juice. A small but meaningful victory.
